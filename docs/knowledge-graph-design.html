Why logistics is its own row, not folded into compliance

Logistics and compliance both care about where a sample physically is and both react to a freezer incident — but their questions differ. Compliance asks "is this allowed, and what is the obligation" (consent, destruction); logistics asks "is the cold chain intact and is this sample on schedule to its next stage." The first is a permission question, the second an operational one — so logistics sits with the primary operational users, sharing the "where is my sample" spine (UC1, UC5) with a custody and cold-chain slant.

Data & project · Section 10

Lab-science primer

What the biology behind the variables actually means, ordered as the sample's journey. The generic lab science here is well-established; anything PM-SC-specific that was inferred is flagged in §11.

10.1 Sample types — how tissue is preserved

  • FFPE — formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded. The standard hospital-pathology preservation: soak in formalin, embed in a paraffin block. Great for microscopy and for DNA; harsh on RNA, because formalin cross-links and fragments molecules. This is why FFPE RNA has worse quality scores — and why the synthetic generator must make FFPE RNA fail more often than fresh-frozen. Stored at room temperature for years.
  • Fresh-Frozen — snap-frozen, often in liquid nitrogen, very soon after removal. Best quality for RNA and protein. Requires an unbroken cold chain — hence every temperature-deviation and transport-temperature field in the schema.
  • Blood — yields plasma, serum, circulating tumour DNA (ctDNA) and germline DNA. Tube type matters because each additive does something different: EDTA (purple) anticoagulant, standard for plasma/DNA and ctDNA · Heparin (green) anticoagulant, inhibits some assays · Citrate (blue) coagulation tests · Serum (red/yellow) no anticoagulant, blood clots and you keep the serum.
  • Biopsy vs resection — a biopsy is a small needle or core sample; a resection is a larger surgical piece. In the schema these appear as sample-type options, which is part of why the taxonomy overlaps.

10.2 Collection and early handling — quality starts here

  • Centrifugation (blood) — spinning to separate plasma/serum from cells. The time from draw to spin matters: delay degrades the analytes. A quality metric disguised as a timestamp.
  • Ischemia and cold chain — time spent warm and unpreserved ("warm ischemia") degrades RNA and protein fast. The four pathology deviation fields capture exactly what ruins a sample: delays, warm excursions, physical damage, mix-ups.

10.3 Pathology and preparation

  • PAD number — patologisk-anatomisk diagnos, the pathology department's specimen identifier.
  • Sectioning (FFPE) — shaving thin slices off the wax block with a microtome; thickness in µm. The "rolls"/scrolls are the curls of tissue collected into a tube.
  • Microdissection — cutting out only the tumour-rich part of a slide, so you analyse tumour rather than surrounding normal tissue. Raises tumour cell content, which downstream assays care about.
  • Cryoprep / cryopulverisation (fresh-frozen) — grinding frozen tissue to a fine powder while keeping it frozen, so it is homogeneous before splitting. That is the "cryopowder" in the fields.
  • AllPrep — a Qiagen kit that co-extracts DNA + RNA + protein from the same sample in one workflow. This is why one specimen fans out into three parallel aliquots in the data.

10.4 Extraction, yield and QC

Purified material is dissolved ("eluted") into a small volume of stabilising buffer. Then: concentration — mass per microlitre, measured with Qubit, a fluorescence assay that is accurate because it binds only the molecule you want; and total yield = concentration × elution volume, i.e. "did we get enough material". Then a QC Pass/Fail decision and, on failure, a fail action: repeat, exclude, or investigate.

Worth knowing — what this schema does NOT record

The wider field uses named purity/integrity scores you will see in the literature: 260/280 and 260/230 ratios (DNA purity), RIN / RINe (RNA Integrity Number, 1–10), and DV200 (% of RNA longer than 200 bases — the key metric for fragile FFPE RNA). This REDCap does not record any of them. It uses concentration + total yield + a Pass/Fail flag instead. Know this so you are not surprised they are absent — and so nothing in the showcase displays them. That is blocker F1 in the Mockup review tab.

10.5 Downstream platforms

  • DNA → Clinical Genomics — sequencing (gene panels / whole genome) to find mutations. Data returns via datafrom_cg.
  • RNA → "Genomics Express" — an RNA analysis destination. What this service actually is remains an open question (§11, Q6).
  • Protein → Clinical Proteomics (mass spectrometry) — peptide digestion chops proteins into shorter peptides (usually with trypsin) because mass specs measure peptides, not whole proteins; SP3 is a bead-based clean-up method; injection amount is how much peptide (ng) is loaded; the mass spectrometer weighs peptides to identify and quantify proteins — models here are TimsTOF (Bruker) and Astral (Thermo); Spectronaut is the software that turns raw signal into protein measurements (a DIA analysis tool).
  • MTBP — Molecular Tumor Board Portal — where clinicians review the combined molecular results to decide treatment. The end of the journey.

13.1 Edge cases the generator must guarantee

  1. A repeat chain of depth ≥ 2 — otherwise the recursion argument is unproven and the repeat query looks like an ordinary two-hop join.
  2. A shared-cause cluster — three failures on the same operator and the same day, so "find similar" returns something.
  3. A storage incident cohort — several samples co-located in one freezer position range on one date, so the impact-analysis case has a blast radius.
  4. A stalled submission — sent to a platform with no data returned.
  5. A blood-only path — a shorter journey that never produces RNA or protein.
  6. An early-stuck specimen — reached pathology and no further.
  7. One patient with two specimens — which is what breaks any funnel that mixes patients and specimens as units.

Those seven are what make the Rendering options and Design report tabs demonstrable rather than describable. Ownership sits with this author, who owns test-data export and evaluation as a WP2 duty.

Section 1

What kind of knowledge graph fits this data

PM-SC's data is an operational provenance graph: a small, densely typed, time-ordered, multi-source record of physical objects being transformed and re-identified as they move between institutions. That is a different animal from the biomedical knowledge graphs in the literature, and the difference drives every subsequent decision.

1.1 First, the definition we are working to

Hogan et al. adopt a deliberately inclusive definition — a knowledge graph is

"a graph of data intended to accumulate and convey knowledge of the real world, whose nodes represent entities of interest and whose edges represent relations between these entities." Hogan et al., Knowledge Graphs, §1 Introduction1

Nicholson & Greene's biomedical narrowing of that is the one PM-SC actually satisfies — and their exclusion criterion is the useful part:

"a resource that integrates one or more expert-derived sources of information into a graph where nodes represent biomedical entities and edges represent relationships between two entities… We do not consider databases like DISEASES and DrugBank to be knowledge graphs. Although these resources contain essential information, they do not represent their data in the form of a graph." Nicholson & Greene 2020, §15

This matters for WP2's framing. REDCap today is not a knowledge graph — it is eight instruments that happen to repeat an ID column. What makes the deliverable a knowledge graph is (a) re-expressing the ID chain as traversable edges and (b) typing the nodes against external vocabularies (the OMOP mapping work). Those two things are the "knowledge" in the name.

1.2 The family it belongs to: a hetnet, not a discovery graph

Structurally, PM-SC is a heterogeneous network in Himmelstein's sense — many node types, many relationship types, aggregated on shared nodes:

"Hetnets (short for heterogeneous networks) are networks with multiple types of nodes and relationships. They offer an intuitive, versatile, and powerful structure for data integration by aggregating graphs for each relationship type onto common nodes." Himmelstein et al. 2017 (Hetionet / Project Rephetio), Introduction4

But the purpose is different, and conflating the two would be the biggest design error available here. The table makes the distinction explicit.

1.3 Data model: labelled property graph, ontology-anchored, RDF-exportable

Hogan surveys the candidate models1. The choice between a directed edge-labelled graph (RDF) and a property graph turns on one question for PM-SC: do edges need attributes? They emphatically do — SUBMITTED_TO {sent_on, volume_µl, returned_on}, PERFORMED {role, date}, STORED_AT {from, to, position}. Hogan is direct about the limitation:

"In the case of directed edge-labelled graphs, we cannot directly annotate an edge like Santiago – flight ➛ Arica with the company (or companies) offering that route… The property graph model was thus proposed to offer additional flexibility when modelling more complex relations… In summary, directed edge-labelled graphs offer a more minimal model, while property graphs offer a more flexible one." Hogan et al., §2.1 Models — Property graphs1

Three further arguments point the same way:

  • Attribute density. A single DNA aliquot carries ~14 scalar fields (elution volume, buffer, concentration, total, QC outcome, fail action, comment, send date, submitted volume, return date, freezer, box, position, repeat-ID). In RDF that is 14 triples per aliquot before any relationship is expressed; in an LPG it is one node with a property bag.
  • Precedent in the exact domain. CKG — the schema backbone already adopted in domain-brief.md §6 — made the same call: "we constructed a graph database that naturally connects large and heterogeneous data. We chose the open-source Neo4J database platform as our current backend because of its performance, industry acceptance and associated Cypher query language"3. Hetionet likewise ships a public Neo4j browser and recommends the Neo4j/JSON distributions precisely because they "include node and edge properties — such as URLs, source and license information, and confidence scores"4.
  • The renderer. Cytoscape.js (the decided stack) is natively LPG-shaped: arbitrary data objects on both nodes and edges, which is what the facet and inspector panels bind to.

The cost of choosing LPG is interoperability, and it is payable rather than fatal: property graphs "can be converted to/from directed edge-labelled graphs"1. So the recommendation is a runtime LPG with an RDF/OMOP export projection — the graph is the working surface, the RDF/BioSamples serialisation is a view over it, generated for the "import from biobanks" and PM Portal feature requirements.

Recommendation 1 — model

A labelled property graph (Neo4j-compatible; Memgraph or an in-process alternative is acceptable for the showcase). Node labels and edge types drawn from a fixed, versioned metagraph. Semantic anchoring by ontology_iri properties (OMOP Person, SNOMED-CT, MIABIS/SPREC for specimens, PROV-O for activities) rather than by native RDF, so the vocabulary work is not blocked on a triplestore. Provide an RDF/JSON-LD export endpoint from day one so the choice stays reversible.

1.4 A provenance graph specifically: adopt PROV-O as the backbone

The PM-SC pipeline is a chain of transformations of physical material by people. There is a standard shape for exactly that, and Hogan names it in the context discussion:

"the PROV Data Model, which specifies how provenance can be described in RDF graphs, where entities (e.g., graphs, nodes, physical document) are derived from other entities, are generated and/or used by activities (e.g., extraction, authorship), and are attributed to agents (e.g., people, software, organisations)." Hogan et al., §3.3 Context — Direct representation1

The mapping onto PM-SC is one-to-one and needs no argument: Entity = Specimen / Aliquot; Activity = sectioning, cryoprep, AllPrep, SP3 digestion, MS injection; Agent = the HSA-ID operator and the facility. This gives the graph a spine that is already standardised, already has a serialisation, and already answers "what was this derived from and who did it" — which is the dashboard's core question. The biological spine (Patient → Biological_sample → Analytical_sample) comes from CKG3; the provenance spine comes from PROV-O. They compose cleanly because they describe different axes.

Section 2

Nodes and edges — the metagraph

Twelve node types and twenty edge types, derived field-by-field from the REDCap dictionary. The hard part is not enumeration — it is deciding what earns promotion to a node, because every unnecessary node type costs the user a legend entry and a filter row.

2.1 The promotion rule

Two independent sources give the same starting heuristic. Himmelstein:

"We abided by a simple litmus test for determining how to encode information in a hetnet: nodes represent nouns, relationships represent verbs." Himmelstein et al. 2017, Materials and methods — Hetionet4

And Bechberger & Perryman, from the modelling side:

"Entity — Commonly represented by nouns, entities describe the things or the type of things in the domain… entities often become vertices. Relationship — Often represented by verbs (or verbal phrases), relationships describe how entities interact with one another… relationships often become edges. Attribute — Like entities, also represented by a noun, but always in the context of an entity or relationship." Bechberger & Perryman, Graph Databases in Action, §2.12

The noun/verb test is necessary but not sufficient — sample_color is a noun and is obviously a property. The operative rule for PM-SC adds two conditions; a field is promoted to a node only if at least one holds:

  1. Sharing — more than one entity points at the same value, so the node becomes a hub you can pivot on (an operator, a freezer, a platform, a deviation type).
  2. Own attributes — the thing has its own dates, actors, or outcomes (a QC result, a platform run, an activity).
  3. Traversal — you need to route through it to answer a question (the identifier chain; storage containment).

Himmelstein also gives the counter-pressure, and it should be applied here: "in Hetionet v1.0, we reduced the number of gene set node types from 14 to 3 by omitting several gene set collections and aggregating all pathway nodes"4. The PM-SC analogue: the dictionary contains six separate operator fields (res_sub_name, prep_sectioning_sign, prep_cryoprep_sign, allprep_operator, prot_extr_operator ×3). These become one Staff metanode, with the role carried as a property on the PERFORMED edge. Six node types collapse to one; the "who touched this sample" query gets simpler, not harder.

The Identifier node — the one contentious call

Identifiers could be properties on Specimen. Promoting them to nodes doubles the node count and can clutter the canvas. The argument for promoting them anyway is that the ID chain is the WP2 deliverable, and Hogan's identity chapter is about precisely this failure mode — merging graphs on ambiguous local names produces silently wrong results, which is why "long-lasting persistent identifiers (PIDs) can be created in order to uniquely identify an entity"1. Two design details in that table are worth naming.

DERIVED_FROM is a materialised shortcut. The PROV-correct expression of "this DNA came from that specimen" is a two-hop path through the AllPrep Activity node. But the most common query in the whole dashboard is "trace this aliquot back to its patient", and forcing every such query through the activity nodes doubles the path length. So store both: the PROV triple for correctness and audit, and a redundant DERIVED_FROM edge for cheap traversal. This is a deliberate denormalisation, and it is the graph equivalent of the read-model pattern Bechberger describes2.

HAS_QUANTIFIED shows exactly where CKG's edge-property idiom lands. CKG stores the measurement on the relationship: "The relationship between analyzed samples and proteins ((Analytical_sample)-[:HAS_QUANTIFIED]-(protein)) will have the quantification (that is, label-free quantification (LFQ) intensity) stored as a property/attribute of the relationship (value)"3. That single sentence is the strongest available argument for the property-graph choice in §1.3, because it is what the PM-SC graph will need the moment omics results come back — and it is not expressible as a plain RDF triple.

2.4 What stayed a property, deliberately

Concentrations, elution volumes, totals, buffer choice, section thickness, roll count, sample colour, all comment fields, and every date that is simply "when this node happened". None of them are shared, none carry their own actors, and none is something you traverse through. Bechberger's validation check applies — "Do I have different vertex or edge labels with the same properties?"2 — and a Concentration node type would fail it immediately.

One borderline case worth flagging for the team: buffer choice (dna_elution_choice, rna_elution_choice) is a closed vocabulary shared across many aliquots, so by rule (1) it qualifies for promotion. It is left as a property here because no question in §7 traverses through it. If a reagent-lot investigation ever becomes a use case, promote it then — which is itself an argument for the graph, since promoting a property to a node requires no migration of the other node types.

3.2 The ten queries that justify the build

Q1 · Forward provenance — "show me everything about this sample"

The single-sample subgraph. This is the staged-build step 1 in domain-brief.md §6, and the most visually convincing thing in the showcase.

MATCH path = (pt:Patient)-[:HAS_SPECIMEN]->(sp:Specimen)<-[:DERIVED_FROM*0..3]-(a)
WHERE (sp)-[:IDENTIFIED_AS]->(:Identifier {value:$anyId})
OPTIONAL MATCH (a)-[:HAS_QC]->(q), (a)-[:SUBMITTED_TO]->(r)-[:RUN_AT]->(f)
RETURN path, q, r, f
Q2 · Cross-system ID resolution — the WP2 deliverable, made executable

Paste any identifier from any system; get the whole chain. Note that the caller does not need to know which system the ID came from, nor how many hops away the answer is.

MATCH (i:Identifier {value:$paste})<-[:IDENTIFIED_AS]-(e)
MATCH (e)-[:IDENTIFIED_AS]->(all:Identifier)
RETURN all.scheme, all.value, all.system
ORDER BY all.system

// and the harder version, once reconciliation edges exist across systems:
MATCH (i:Identifier {value:$paste})-[:SAME_MATERIAL_AS*1..4]-(j:Identifier)
RETURN DISTINCT j.system, j.scheme, j.value

That second query is a regular path query — Hogan: "A key feature that distinguishes graph query languages is the ability to include path expressions in queries… a regular expression that allows for matching arbitrary-length paths between two nodes"1. There is no fixed-arity SQL equivalent because the number of systems an ID traverses is not known in advance.

Q3 · Backward impact / recall — "what did this contaminate?"

A freezer alarm on 2026-04-11, or a mislabelled rack, or a reagent lot withdrawn. Which patients, which aliquots, which already-delivered results are affected? This is the query that is genuinely hard to write against the current REDCap export and trivial here.

MATCH (loc:StorageLocation {label:$freezer})-[:CONTAINS*0..3]->(pos)
      <-[st:STORED_AT]-(item)
WHERE st.from <= date($incident) AND (st.to IS NULL OR st.to >= date($incident))
MATCH (item)-[:DERIVED_FROM*0..3]->(sp:Specimen)<-[:HAS_SPECIMEN]-(pt:Patient)
OPTIONAL MATCH (item)-[:SUBMITTED_TO]->(r:PlatformRun)
WHERE r.data_returned_on IS NOT NULL
RETURN pt.study_id, collect(DISTINCT item.id) AS affected,
       collect(DISTINCT r.kind)  AS results_already_delivered
Q4 · Repeat chains — the unbounded recursion
MATCH chain = (latest:AnalyticalSample)-[:REPEAT_OF*]->(original:AnalyticalSample)
WHERE NOT ()-[:REPEAT_OF]->(latest)
MATCH (latest)-[:HAS_QC]->(q:QCResult)
RETURN original.id, length(chain)+1 AS attempts, q.outcome AS final_outcome
ORDER BY attempts DESC

This is Bechberger's motivating case verbatim: "Relational databases do not handle recursive operations (especially unbounded ones) well, struggling both with syntax and performance"2. Nobody knows a priori whether a sample was repeated once or four times.

Q5 · Cohort assembly by pattern — the domain-brief's own example

"Female patients >65 whose RNA failed QC, was repeated, and whose retry reached Genomics Express."

MATCH (pt:Patient)-[:HAS_SPECIMEN]->(sp)<-[:DERIVED_FROM]-(rna:AnalyticalSample {molecule:'RNA'})
      -[:HAS_QC]->(:QCResult {outcome:'Fail'}),
      (retry:AnalyticalSample)-[:REPEAT_OF]->(rna),
      (retry)-[:SUBMITTED_TO]->(:PlatformRun)-[:RUN_AT]->(f:Facility {name:'Genomics Express'})
WHERE pt.sex = 'Female' AND pt.age_at_enroll > 65
RETURN pt.study_id, rna.id, retry.id
Q6 · Gap detection — querying for a missing edge

Operationally the most-used query in the whole tool: what is stuck? It is a pattern that asserts the absence of a relationship.

// aliquots sent to a platform with no data back after 30 days
MATCH (a:AnalyticalSample)-[s:SUBMITTED_TO]->(r:PlatformRun)
WHERE r.data_returned_on IS NULL AND s.sent_on < date() - duration('P30D')
RETURN a.id, r.kind, duration.inDays(s.sent_on, date()).days AS days_waiting
ORDER BY days_waiting DESC

// specimens that reached PM-SC but produced no aliquot at all
MATCH (sp:Specimen)-[:IDENTIFIED_AS]->(:Identifier {scheme:'pmsc_id'})
WHERE NOT (sp)<-[:DERIVED_FROM]-(:AnalyticalSample)
RETURN sp
Q7 · Shared-cause detection — the batch-effect pattern

Failures that share an operator and a processing day. Bechberger files this under pattern matching: "Does this transaction look like other known fraudulent transactions?"2 — same shape, different domain.

MATCH (st:Staff)-[:PERFORMED]->(act:Activity)-[:GENERATED]->(a:AnalyticalSample)
      -[:HAS_QC]->(:QCResult {outcome:'Fail'})
WITH st, act.date AS day, collect(a.id) AS failures
WHERE size(failures) >= 2
RETURN st.hsa_id, day, failures, size(failures) AS n
ORDER BY n DESC
Q8 · Shortest connection — "how are these two things related?"
MATCH p = shortestPath( (a {id:$idA})-[*..8]-(b {id:$idB}) )
RETURN p

Useful as an interaction (select two nodes → "connect"), and as the answer to the recurring question "why is this MS run showing up in this patient's record?"

Q9 · Deviation-weighted quality

Does a recorded deviation actually predict QC failure? A question that spans Specimen → Deviation and Specimen → Aliquot → QC in one pattern.

MATCH (sp:Specimen)<-[:DERIVED_FROM]-(a:AnalyticalSample)-[:HAS_QC]->(q:QCResult)
OPTIONAL MATCH (sp)-[:HAS_DEVIATION]->(d:Deviation)
WITH a.molecule AS molecule, coalesce(d.type,'none') AS deviation, q.outcome AS outcome
RETURN molecule, deviation, outcome, count(*) AS n
ORDER BY molecule, deviation

Note the honesty flag. Q9 ends in an aggregation, so by Bechberger's verdict the counting belongs in the relational side. The graph's contribution is the pattern — assembling the deviation↔outcome pairs across four node types. In the dashboard this renders as a chart, not as a node-link diagram. Compare Hetionet, where "this single query spans four node and five relationship types"4 and still returns a ranked list.

Q10 · The metagraph query — data-model self-description
MATCH (a)-[r]->(b)
RETURN labels(a)[0] AS from, type(r) AS edge, labels(b)[0] AS to, count(*) AS n
ORDER BY n DESC

This one query powers the schema map, the legend, the facet counts and the "is anything unexpectedly empty?" data-quality check. It is also the demo opener: the graph describing itself.

3.3 Query connectivity — why length matters

Himmelstein reports a property of hetnets that transfers directly:

"Among the 11 metanodes, there are 66 possible source–target pairs. However, only 11 of them have at least one direct connection. In contrast, for paths of length 2, 50 pairs have connectivity… At length 3, all 66 pairs are connected." Himmelstein et al. 2017, Results4

PM-SC's metagraph has 13 metanodes → 78 ordered pairs, of which roughly 20 are directly connected. Almost every interesting question is therefore a 2–4 hop question: Staff → QCResult (3 hops), StorageLocation → Patient (4 hops), Deviation → PlatformRun (4 hops). None of these pairs has a table to join in REDCap; all of them are one MATCH in the graph. That gap — between "directly connected" and "connected at length 3" — is quantitatively where the graph earns its place.

3.4 Deliberately deferred

  • Centrality / community detection. Hogan lists them among the standard analytics — degree, betweenness, PageRank, Louvain1. At a few hundred samples, "the operator with the highest degree" is just "the operator who works most", which is a bar chart. Revisit at multi-study scale.
  • Metapath scoring. Hetionet's DWPC over 1,206 metapaths4 is the right tool for asking which processing routes predict failure — but it needs thousands of labelled outcomes. Note it as a roadmap item, do not build it.
  • Embeddings / GNNs. Out of scope. Named here only so that the question "why aren't you doing ML on it?" has a prepared answer: the graph is small and its edges are known rather than predicted.

Section 4

Insights a graph gives that a relational database does not

This section is deliberately two-sided. A design report that only lists advantages will not survive contact with a sceptical developer, and PM-SC already has a relational system of record that is not going anywhere. The recommendation is a hybrid — and the case for the graph is stronger for being bounded.

4.1 The size of the claim

"For every 100 questions modern applications answer, the most common data tool these use (namely, a relational database) handles only about 88 of those questions well. That leaves 12 types of questions where relational databases struggle. These remaining questions deal with the links and connections within the data." Bechberger & Perryman, §1 Introduction2

That is the right order of magnitude to claim, and PM-SC's 12% are unusually valuable ones: they are exactly the provenance and impact questions that a sample-tracking infrastructure exists to answer.

4.2 Seven things the graph gives you

(1) Traversal of unknown length

The ID chain has five links today; it will have six when the Omics Leaderboard is linked and seven when IDS User Portal arrives. Repeat chains have unknown depth. Storage is nested to arbitrary depth. Hogan puts the capability precisely:

"Unlike (other) NoSQL models, specialised graph query languages support not only standard relational operators (joins, unions, projections, etc.), but also navigational operators for recursively finding entities connected through arbitrary-length paths." Hogan et al., §1 Introduction1

Bechberger's diagnostic question is the one to put to the team: "Attempting to perform this query in a relational database requires an unknown number of joins… If a recursive approach helps to solve the problem, then a graph database is often preferable."2

(2) Schema-late integration across eight systems

Hogan's opening parable is, almost word for word, the PM-SC situation. A tourism board designs a relational schema, then discovers events have multiple names, multiple venues, unknown dates:

"Along the way, the board has to incrementally change the schema several times in order to support new sources of data. Each such change requires a costly remodelling, reloading, and reindexing of data… The tourism board struggles with the relational model because they do not know, a priori, what data will need to be modelled or what sources they will use." Hogan et al., §2.1 Models1

PM-SC's landscape (domain-brief.md §9) is REDCap, Sympathy → LifeCare, LabVantage+Labware, FreezerPro, the Omics Leaderboard, Clinical Genomics, Clinical Proteomics, MTBP, BioSamples, IDS User Portal. Several of these do not exist yet or are mid-migration. Adding a source to the graph means adding node and edge types; existing nodes and existing queries are untouched. PrimeKG's authors describe the same integration problem from the vocabulary side — "database developers select the ontology that best suits their function from a multitude of biorepositories… their disease encodings overlap unsystematically and are often in conflict"6 — and their answer, integrating 20 resources into one graph with a harmonisation layer, is structurally the same answer.

(3) Relationships become first-class, attributed, searchable objects

"Which submissions to Clinical Genomics took more than 14 days to return data?" is a query over edges. In the relational model that fact lives as two date columns on a form and you must already know which form to look in. In the graph, SUBMITTED_TO is a thing you can enumerate, filter, colour, and count without touching the nodes at either end. CKG's HAS_QUANTIFIED {value}3 is the same move at the biology layer.

(4) Forward and backward are the same query

Provenance ("where did this come from?") and impact ("what did this affect?") are the same pattern with the arrows reversed — one MATCH, one character changed. In SQL they are two different query trees, each written and maintained separately. For an infrastructure whose reason to exist is accountability, having recall analysis come free with provenance is not a minor convenience.

(5) Absence is queryable

WHERE NOT (sp)<-[:DERIVED_FROM]-() finds every specimen that never produced an aliquot. The relational equivalent — a LEFT JOIN … WHERE x IS NULL across the right pair of forms — is writable but requires knowing in advance which pair. In the graph, "find every entity missing an outgoing edge of type T" is a generic query, which means the stuck-sample detector can be driven from the metagraph rather than hand-written per stage. That is the difference between a report and a monitor.

(6) Class-level questions answer themselves

With a semantic schema, FFPE ⊑ TissueSpecimen ⊑ Specimen means a filter on "tissue" returns FFPE and fresh-frozen without the analyst enumerating the values1. This directly mitigates open Q3 from data-inventory.md — the sample_type/sample_type1 overlap where FFPE is a processing state and Biopsy a collection method. Rather than forcing a taxonomy cleanup before the dashboard ships, model the two axes as two class hierarchies and let the graph carry the mess honestly.

(7) Source and trust become part of the model
"It may be beneficial to manage multiple graphs from different sources, making it possible to update or refine data from one source, to distinguish untrustworthy sources from more trustworthy ones." Hogan et al., §2.1.4 Graph dataset1

For the showcase this is immediately practical: synthetic nodes and real nodes can live in the same graph in different named subgraphs, and the UI can render the synthetic ones distinctly. It also gives the November demo its integrity guarantee — the honesty caveat becomes a data property, not a disclaimer slide.

4.3 Four things the graph does not give you

Where the relational database wins
  • Aggregation. "Relational databases are optimized to perform complex aggregation queries quickly and with a minimal amount of overhead… These same sorts of queries can be performed in graph databases, but the nature of graph traversals requires that much more of the data is touched… VERDICT: For these types of questions, use an RDBMS."2 The CONSORT funnel, QC pass rates, turnaround medians and the swimmer plot are all aggregation. They belong in View A, served from SQL.
  • Attribute search. Finding a record by ID or scanning free-text comments is an index problem. Bechberger's verdict: "use an RDBMS or search technology."2
  • Flexibility alone is not an argument. "Flexibility with data schema alone should not be a sufficient reason to choose a graph database"2. PM-SC's case rests on (1), (4) and (5) above — traversal, bidirectionality, absence — not on schema convenience.
  • A natural graph shape is not an argument either. Bechberger reports a client whose domain "modelled naturally in a graph" — the customer's own objects were literally called components and relationships — yet the right answer was a read-optimised relational projection, which delivered "a 100-fold performance improvement"2. The lesson is exact: model shape does not decide; access patterns decide. Ours are traversal-heavy, so the graph is justified — but the reasoning must be stated that way, not as "samples form a graph."

SQL — 5 joins, a self-join, and prior knowledge of the form layout

       r2.pmscid_rna AS retry
FROM   research_subjects_enrollment e

       ON sc.record_id = e.record_id
JOIN   pmsc_sample_registration pr

       ON r1.pmsc_id_v2 = pr.pmsc_id

       ON r2.rna_repeat = r1.pmscid_rna
WHERE  e.res_sub_sex      = 'Female'
  AND  e.res_sub_exactage > 65
  AND  r1.qc_rna          = 'Fail'

-- and this still cannot express "reached Genomics
-- Express" without knowing that send_rna is the
-- Genomics Express column, nor handle a sample
-- repeated twice.
Cypher — one pattern, and repeat depth is free
MATCH (pt:Patient)-[:HAS_SPECIMEN]->(sp)
      <-[:DERIVED_FROM]-(rna {molecule:'RNA'})
      -[:HAS_QC]->(:QCResult {outcome:'Fail'}),
      (retry)-[:REPEAT_OF*1..]->(rna),
      (retry)-[:SUBMITTED_TO]->(:PlatformRun)
      -[:RUN_AT]->(:Facility
                   {name:'Genomics Express'})
WHERE pt.sex = 'Female'
  AND pt.age_at_enroll > 65
RETURN pt.study_id, rna.id AS failed,
       retry.id AS retry;

The difference that matters is not line count. It is [:REPEAT_OF*1..]: the graph handles a sample repeated three times with no change to the query, and the SQL cannot without another self-join or a recursive CTE. It is also that the Cypher names the destination facility rather than a column that happens to mean the destination — which is the semantic layer doing its job.

4.5 The recommended architecture

Recommendation 2 — hybrid, graph as a derived layer

REDCap (and later the LIMS/platform APIs) remain the system of record. The graph is a derived, rebuildable projection refreshed on a cadence — never hand-edited, never the place data is entered. Aggregate/statistical views (View A) are served from a relational or columnar store built from the same export. The graph serves traversal, provenance, gap detection and exploration (View B).

This is CKG's architecture too — a Neo4j graph plus an independent Python analytics core, where "both modules are independent of the CKG database"3 — and it is Bechberger's explicit advice: "switching to using a graph database does not have to be an all-or-nothing situation… Multi-model approaches with graph databases are common and, in our experience, tend to be very successful."2

Consequence for the showcase: since the graph is rebuilt from an export, the synthetic-to-live switch really is a data-source change, as the brief requires. It also means a bad graph build is never a data-loss event.

5.3 Interaction semantics — three rules that must be got right

Rule 1 — OR within a facet, AND across facets. Selecting FFPE and Fresh-Frozen widens; adding QC = Fail narrows. Standard, but it must be stated because graph UIs frequently get it backwards.

Rule 2 — filter, then re-expand along paths. This is the rule specific to graphs and the one that decides whether the view is usable. If the user filters to "QC = Fail", a naïve implementation hides every node that is not a QCResult and renders a field of disconnected dots. The correct behaviour is:

  1. evaluate the predicate to get a seed set;
  2. re-expand to the connected neighbourhood — by default, the full provenance path of each seed (patient ↔ seed ↔ platform), plus 1 hop;
  3. render seeds at full opacity and context nodes dimmed.

The user's mental model is "show me the failures and how they got there", never "show me failure nodes in isolation." Expose the expansion radius as a visible control so the behaviour is never a surprise.

Rule 3 — counts are computed on the filtered graph, and shown even at zero. A facet value with count 0 must render greyed rather than disappear; vanishing options destroy the user's sense of what the data contains. Counts are one MATCH … RETURN count(*) per facet against the current filter — batch them into a single round trip, which is the same aggregation query as §3.2 Q10.

Facet state is a URL

Encode the full facet state in the query string. It makes any view shareable in an email — which is how a "this sample is stuck" message will actually be sent — and it makes the November demo scriptable and reproducible rather than a live-clicking gamble.

6.3 Layout: layered, not force-directed

Recommendation 3 — lock the x-axis to pipeline stage

The PM-SC graph is a time-ordered DAG. Force-directed layout (Cytoscape's cose, fcose) optimises for edge-crossing minimisation and produces a hairball that discards the one structure every user already knows: material flows left to right through stages. Use a layered layout (dagre or elk with rankDir: LR), with rank pinned to pipeline stage. Nodes then land in the same column as the process diagram in domain-brief.md §5, and the user's existing mental model transfers for free.

Reserve force-directed for one case: the similarity neighbourhood ("what else looks like this failure?"), where there is no time axis and clustering is the point.

Encoding discipline, so the canvas stays readable:

  • Hue = node type, from the palette already in the mockup. Nothing else uses hue.
  • Stroke = state. Red ring = QC fail; dashed ring = stalled; grey fill = filtered-out context.
  • Shape = coarse class (circle person, square specimen, diamond aliquot, triangle run) so the view survives greyscale printing and colour-vision differences.
  • Edge weight = count in aggregate mode only. Never encode two variables on one channel.
  • No node labels below ~40 nodes on screen; labels on hover and on selection. Labels are the primary cause of hairball illegibility.

6.4 Scale management: progressive disclosure through the quotient graph

Three zoom levels, and the transition between them is the quotient/expand operation:

  1. Cohort level — the metagraph with instance counts on each metanode and metaedge. Always the default landing state. Never more than 13 nodes.
  2. Group level — one metanode expanded into its instances, others still collapsed. E.g. all 151 aliquots against collapsed Patient/Platform hubs.
  3. Instance level — full node-link, bounded to ≤ ~1,500 visible elements, which is roughly where Cytoscape.js interaction latency becomes noticeable on a laptop.

High-degree hub nodes (an operator with 200 edges, the Clinical Genomics facility) render as a collapsed chip with a count badge that expands on demand. Without this, two or three hubs consume the entire canvas and every layout algorithm produces a star.

6.6 Idiom discipline

domain-brief.md §6 already states the rule and it is worth restating as a design constraint: node-link for cross-entity relationships, timeline for one journey, matrix for sample × QC, chart for counting. Concretely, four projections of the same underlying graph:

Verdict, and what the mockup already gets right

Verdict

Calling these "very basic mockups" undersells them. 806 lines, one file, zero build, four working tabs, a hand-written 3D graph engine with three layout modes, and a timed nine-lane custodian swimlane. The information architecture is already the right one — it matches §6.1 of the design report without having been designed against it. The gap is not ambition; it is fidelity and connection. Nothing links to anything else, and a handful of details would be spotted immediately by the WP1 lab staff and cost credibility in the room.

The work below is mostly subtraction and reconciliation, not new construction. There is one genuine architectural question (3D vs 2D) and one genuine strategic one (how much of the §2 metagraph to implement before November).

What is already good

  • The Workflow swimlane is the best artefact in the project. Nine custodian lanes (coordination · OR · pathology · logistics · PMSC lab · SciLifeLab ×3 · MTB), duration-proportional bars, a real fan-out at AllPrep into three parallel platform lanes and a fan-in to MTB, dashed hand-off curves with arrowheads, a playhead, and — the detail that shows care — the ≈ prefix on bars whose width was capped (index.html:162, 728). It encodes custodian × time × phase × duration on four orthogonal channels and is honest about its own compressed scale. That is better than most production dashboards.
  • The "active handling vs total elapsed" split (index.html:716, 792–794) with the note that most elapsed time is platform analysis, not hands-on work. This is exactly the kind of framing that stops a stakeholder drawing the wrong conclusion from a long bar.
  • Failure is visible. repeat_of edges render orange and thicker (index.html:647); QC-failed nodes get a red ring (index.html:657). The domain brief insists failure and repeat are in the data; the mockup honours that rather than showing a clean happy path.
  • The retry is modelled correctly. link(rep, al, 'repeat_of') points the retry at the original, and the retry is also linked to the parent sample (index.html:533–534). That is the same direction and shape as the report's REPEAT_OF metaedge. No change needed.
  • Deterministic synthetic data. A seeded LCG (index.html:498) means the demo cohort is reproducible run to run. Small thing; the difference between a scriptable demo and a gamble.
  • The mock banner is honest (index.html:180) — "non-functional, dummy PreDDLung data" — which is precisely what the domain brief's honesty caveat requires.
  • Tabs are real <button> elements with lazy Mermaid init and a canvas resize on activation. Unglamorous and correct.

Review · Section 4

The three decisions worth arguing about

4.1 Should the graph be 3D?

The canvas engine is real work — perspective projection, depth sorting, depth fog, hover picking in screen space, three easing layout modes. It looks impressive on first contact, which for a showcase is not nothing.

But for this graph the answer is no, not as the default, for four reasons:

  1. The data is a time-ordered DAG. Depth adds a dimension the data does not have, while destroying the one users already understand — left-to-right flow.
  2. Depth ambiguity defeats the primary task. "Which aliquot does this edge reach?" cannot be answered reliably when edges cross in projection and the answer changes as the scene auto-rotates.
  3. Fog dims exactly what matters. fog = max(.25, min(1, 900/zc − .15)) can render a QC-failed node at 25% opacity purely because of where it landed in Z.
  4. The code already concedes it. Selecting "Layered · pipeline" eases rotY→0 and rotX→−0.05 — it flattens to 2D. The most useful mode is the one that turns the 3D off.
Recommendation

Default to 2D layered. Keep the 3D rotating force view as an explicitly-labelled Explore mode, and consider a fifth use for it: an unattended attract loop for a poster session or a lobby screen, where "looks alive" is the actual requirement and no one needs to trace an edge. That reframes the engine from a liability into an asset with a defined job.

4.2 How much of the metagraph before November?

Implementing all 13 metanodes is not the goal. The goal is that the two use cases the milestone is judged on can actually be performed live. That is a much smaller ask:

  • Identifier nodes → UC10. Roughly 4 nodes per sample, one edge type, and an inspector block that is already sketched (and currently hard-coded). This is the deliverable made tangible; it should be the demo's closing move.
  • StorageLocation nodes with a CONTAINS hierarchy → UC5. One nested node type plus a dated STORED_AT edge unlocks the freezer-incident story, which is the most persuasive thing the graph can do and is impossible to fake with a chart.

Deviation, Activity, PlatformRun and QCResult-as-a-node can wait. They deepen UC3 and UC4 but neither is the milestone.

4.3 Keep the hand-rolled engine, or move to Cytoscape.js now?

Two honest considerations pull in opposite directions. The dual objective in the domain brief is (1) ship the showcase and (2) learn frontend development — and writing a graph renderer from scratch has already taught more than wiring a library would. Against that: F16's O(n²) ceiling, and the fact that the decided stack is SvelteKit + Cytoscape.js, so the canvas engine is throwaway code either way.

The resolution is a sequencing one rather than a either/or. Keep the canvas mockup as the design reference — it is the specification for what the real view should feel like, and it is already better looking than a default Cytoscape render. Build the SvelteKit version against Cytoscape + dagre, and port the good decisions across: the layered columns, the operator row along the bottom, the orange repeat_of edges, the red QC ring, the hull-per-type grouped mode. None of those are engine-specific. The mockup's value was never the renderer; it was the encoding decisions.

Strengths

Metagraph — every instance collapsed to its type

"What kinds of things are in here, how many, and how do they connect?"

Strengths

Rendering · I

3D force-directed — attract mode

I

3D rotating graph — the whole cohort at once

"What does all of it look like?" — and, for a demo audience, "is this thing alive?"

drag to rotate · hover a node
Strengths

Genuinely beautiful, and it does one analytical job well: at a glance you see that the graph has a shape — a dense specimen/aliquot core, three platform hubs pulling clusters toward them, and the repeat chain hanging off to one side. It communicates "this is an integrated whole" faster than any static diagram, which is worth real money in a funding conversation.

Depth also buys separation that 2D cannot: 36 nodes and 44 edges here would overlap badly on a plane.

Limits

Position carries no meaning, and it changes as the scene rotates — so no spatial memory, no reliable edge tracing, and occlusion hides whatever happens to be behind something. Depth fog dims nodes for geometric reasons rather than semantic ones, so a QC failure can be the faintest thing on screen.

Hit-testing is in projected screen space, so what you click depends on the current camera. Text is unreadable at depth. No CSS, no DOM, no screen reader.

Try this

Press Flatten to pipeline. The camera rotates to face front and the nodes settle into stage columns — and the graph becomes readable. That is the argument in one gesture: the useful mode is the one that turns the 3D off.

The mockup's own code does this already, easing rotY→0 and rotX→−0.05 whenever the layered layout is picked.

Verdict — keep it, but give it a job. Not the working view (that is panel A), and not the default. Its real role is attract mode: the opening ten seconds of the November demo, a poster or conference-booth loop, a lobby screen, the hero image on the PM Portal landing page. Scope it there explicitly in the spec so nobody tries to do analysis in it — and so nobody deletes it either, because as a piece of communication it earns its place.
Implementation — if this is carried into the real build

This demo is hand-rolled: deterministic 3D force layout precomputed once (400 iterations of repulsion + spring + centring), then only the camera rotates each frame — so per-frame cost is one projection and a depth sort, not a physics step. Rendering is Canvas 2D with painter's-algorithm depth sorting, perspective divide (scale = f / (z + camDist)), distance fog and additive glow via shadowBlur. That is the same technique as mockups/index.html, which is worth saying plainly: the mockup's engine is the right approach for this job.

If you want it richer: 3d-force-graph (Vasco Asturiano) wraps three.js plus d3-force-3d and gives you WebGL rendering, bloom post-processing, node sprites, curved links, directional particles and camera fly-to in a few lines. three.js directly if you want control over materials and lighting. Both are heavy dependencies (~150 kB+ gzipped for three.js) — acceptable for an attract loop, hard to justify inside the operational bundle, so code-split it behind a dynamic import and load it only when attract mode is entered.

Non-negotiable: auto-rotation must stop on prefers-reduced-motion: reduce, on hover, and when the view is off screen — continuous orbital motion is a common vestibular trigger, and this is a hospital audience.

9.2 Encoding rules, applied consistently across all eight

  • Hue = node type, and nothing else uses hue. The ten colours in the key at the top are the entire vocabulary.
  • Red/orange = trouble, always: a failed QC, a deviation, a repeat edge, a stalled cell. It is the only semantic colour that crosses type boundaries, and it earns that because "something is wrong" is the one thing a user must never miss.
  • Dashed = derived or inferred: DERIVED_FROM shortcuts, REPEAT_OF, hand-off curves. Solid edges are asserted facts.
  • Position is meaningful wherever possible — x is pipeline stage in A, time in D and E, and containment depth in G. Panel C is the only one where position means nothing, which is exactly the argument against it.
  • Every view has a table equivalent. Panel F is nearly one already; the others need an export.

Interaction & motion

Behaviours, named and spec-ready

The Rendering options tab answered what to draw. This tab answers what it does when you touch it — and, more importantly, gives each behaviour its proper name. A spec that says "make the graph animate nicely" produces an argument; a spec that says "animated layout transition, 600 ms ease-in-out, object constancy preserved by node id, staged so position resolves before colour" produces the thing you wanted.

How to use these cards

Every card has four parts: a live demo you can poke, the technical terms (in monospace — these are the words to put in the ticket), the library and specific API that implements it, and a spec sentence in italics that you can paste into a requirement and hand to Karthick, Sebastian or Saman with acceptance criteria attached. Cards marked in red are techniques I recommend against for this project, included because they will be proposed and you should be able to say why not.

10.1 Layout & transition

X1
Animated layout transition
layout transition · object constancy · staged animation · easing curve · tween · FLIP
What it is

The same nodes move to new positions instead of being destroyed and redrawn. Object constancy is the property that makes it work: a node keeps its identity across the change, so the eye can track it. Without it, switching layout is a jump-cut and the user loses their place.

Turn on stagger to see staged animation — Heer & Robertson's finding that separating a transition into ordered phases is significantly easier to follow than moving everything at once.

Implement with

cy.layout({name:'dagre', animate:true, animationDuration:700, animationEasing:'ease-in-out-cubic'}).run() — Cytoscape keys by element id, so constancy is free.

Outside the graph: Svelte's animate:flip directive (the FLIP technique — First, Last, Invert, Play) for reordering lists and table rows; svelte/motion's tweened with d3-ease curves for scalars.

Layout engines: cytoscape-dagre (fast, good enough) or cytoscape-elk (slower, far better edge routing). Both implement the Sugiyama framework: layer assignment → crossing minimisation → coordinate assignment.

Spec language

"Switching layout MUST animate node positions over 500–800 ms with an ease-in-out cubic curve, preserving object constancy by element id. Nodes MUST NOT be removed and re-added. Where more than ~150 nodes are visible the transition MAY be skipped in favour of an instant redraw. The transition MUST be disabled when prefers-reduced-motion: reduce is set."

Accept when: recording the transition at 60 fps shows every node on a continuous path from old to new position, and no frame drops below 30 fps at 150 nodes.

X2
Path trace — animated provenance walk
line-draw animation · stroke-dashoffset · path interpolation · getPointAtLength · narrative sequencing
What it is

The trace to origin / trace to results context-menu actions from report §6.5, drawn rather than jumped to. The path draws itself and each node lights as the front reaches it, so the user sees the direction of provenance rather than just its endpoints.

This is the single highest-impact animation in the whole product for demo purposes: it makes an abstract traversal legible in two seconds.

Implement with

Classic SVG line-draw: set stroke-dasharray and stroke-dashoffset to path.getTotalLength(), then animate the offset to 0 via the Web Animations API (el.animate([...], {duration, easing})) or Svelte's transition:draw.

Node timing: schedule from path.getPointAtLength(t·L), or simply stagger by index.

The underlying query is the Cypher in §3.2 Q1/Q3 — cy.elements().dijkstra() or cy.elements().bfs() gives the client-side path if you would rather not round-trip.

Spec language

"Selecting trace to origin MUST highlight the full provenance path from the selected element to its root Patient, drawing the path progressively over 1.2–1.8 s and dimming all non-path elements to 15% opacity. A skip affordance MUST complete the animation immediately. The final state MUST be reachable without animation."

Accept when: the end state is identical whether the animation played or was skipped.

10.2 Navigation — overview, focus, detail

X3
Semantic zoom / level of detail
semantic zoom (≠ geometric zoom) · level of detail (LOD) · quotient collapse · zoom thresholds · Shneiderman's mantra
What it is

Geometric zoom makes everything bigger. Semantic zoom changes the representation: at cohort level you see 13 metanode chips with counts, at group level you see clusters, at instance level you see individual nodes with labels. Report §6.4's three levels are literally this.

The collapsed state is Hogan's quotient graph, so the technique has a formal guarantee behind it, not just a UI convention.

Implement with

cy.on('zoom', …) with hysteresis thresholds (e.g. collapse below 0.6, expand above 0.9 — never the same number, or it flickers at the boundary).

cytoscape-expand-collapse for compound-node collapse; cytoscape-layout-utilities for placing the collapsed clusters. Style swaps go through classes, not inline style, so they are batchable.

Overview+detail companion: cytoscape-navigator gives a minimap with a draggable viewport rectangle.

Spec language

"The graph canvas MUST implement three semantic zoom levels — cohort, group, instance — with hysteresis between thresholds so no oscillation occurs at a boundary. Level MUST also be settable explicitly by a control, not only by scroll. Node labels MUST render only at instance level and only when fewer than 40 nodes are in the viewport."

X4
Focus + context (fisheye)
fisheye distortion · degree of interest (DOI) · focus+context · Furnas 1986 · magnification factor
← move the pointer across the strip
What it is

Magnify what is under the pointer while keeping the rest on screen, compressed. Solves the classic tension between seeing detail and keeping context — you never lose your place, because nothing is scrolled away.

Degree of interest is the underlying idea: every element has a computed interest score (distance from focus + intrinsic importance), and the rendering allocates space by that score.

Implement with

d3-fisheye (Bostock's plugin) or hand-rolled — the cartesian distortion is one function of normalised distance. Apply on pointermove, throttled to requestAnimationFrame.

For a graph rather than a strip, the practical alternative is DOI-based dimming (X5) which is far cheaper and less disorienting.

Spec language

"OPTIONAL. If implemented, fisheye MUST be opt-in via a toggle, MUST NOT alter hit-testing (a click MUST select the element the user sees under the cursor), and MUST be disabled under prefers-reduced-motion."

My advice: spec it as a stretch goal. Users find it delightful in demos and disorienting in daily use. DOI dimming gets 80% of the benefit for 10% of the risk.

10.3 Selection & highlighting

X5
k-hop expansion with DOI dimming
ego network · k-hop neighbourhood · BFS ripple · degree-of-interest dimming · halo · progressive disclosure
What it is

Select a node, reveal its neighbourhood one ring at a time, dim everything else. The ego network is the k-hop subgraph around a focus node — the single most useful navigational primitive in any graph UI, and the mechanism behind the "expand 1▸2▸3 hops" control in the report's §6.2 wireframe.

The ripple is not decoration: staging the rings by distance is what tells the user how far something is from their focus.

Implement with

cy.$id(x).closedNeighborhood(), or cy.elements().bfs({roots, visit}) for distance-labelled rings. Apply .addClass('faded') to the complement — never inline styles, so Cytoscape can batch the style recalculation.

Wrap bulk changes in cy.batch(() => {…}); without it, each class change triggers a restyle and the ripple stutters at a few hundred nodes.

Spec language

"Double-clicking a node MUST expand its 1-hop neighbourhood. A control MUST allow expansion to 2 and 3 hops. Non-neighbourhood elements MUST be dimmed to 15% opacity rather than hidden, so context is retained. Expansion MUST be reversible by a single reset action, and MUST push a history entry so browser Back works."

X6
Edge bundling
hierarchical edge bundling (Holten 2006) · beta parameter · curveBundle · edge routing · clutter reduction
What it is

Route edges that share endpoints along a common corridor, trading precise traceability for a large reduction in visual clutter. β is the blend: 0 = straight lines, 1 = fully bundled through the hierarchy.

Drag the slider slowly. Around β≈0.7 the "how many samples go to Clinical Genomics" question becomes much easier and the "which specific aliquot is this edge" question becomes much harder. That trade is the entire decision.

Implement with

d3.curveBundle.beta(0.85) over a path through a hierarchy from d3-hierarchy; or cytoscape.js-edge-bundling; or simply route beziers through a shared control point per source/target group, which is what this demo does.

Spec language

"OPTIONAL, and only at cohort zoom level. Bundling MUST NOT be applied when individual edge traceability is required (provenance trace, ID chain). If implemented, β MUST be user-adjustable and default to 0."

My advice: our graph is sparse and near-tree-shaped. Bundling pays off on dense many-to-many graphs; here it mostly hides the one thing worth seeing. Keep it out of v1.

10.4 Time

X7
Temporal scrubbing — the "as of" control
temporal brushing · time-travel state · playhead · animated transition between temporal states · valid-time query
What it is

Replay the graph as it stood on any past date. Nodes appear when they were created, QC results resolve, aliquots go from submitted to returned. Because every edge in the model carries a timestamp (report §2.3), this is a filter — not a separate data set.

This is the visible form of the "as of" facet from §5.1(G), and it implements Hogan's notion of context as "the scope of truth". It is also the control that makes UC5's freezer-incident query answerable: set the date to the incident, then trace.

Implement with

A single reactive Svelte store ($asOf) that every view derives from — the graph query, the matrix, the KPIs. Server-side it is one extra predicate: WHERE e.from <= $asOf AND (e.to IS NULL OR e.to >= $asOf).

Debounce the slider (~120 ms) so scrubbing does not fire a request per pixel; render optimistically from the client-side cache while the request is in flight.

Spec language

"A global as of date control MUST be present in the application header and MUST apply to every view simultaneously. Changing it MUST update all views within 300 ms. The current value MUST be encoded in the URL. When set to any date other than today, a persistent visual indicator MUST make the historical state unmistakable."

Accept when: a shared URL reproduces the exact historical state for another user.

X8
Flow particles along edges
animated flow marker · animateMotion · getPointAtLength · particle system · perceived throughput
What it is

Dots travelling along edges to suggest movement. It is the single most requested "make it look alive" effect and it will be proposed for the November demo.

Implement with

SVG <animateMotion> with <mpath>, or a requestAnimationFrame loop sampling path.getPointAtLength(t·L) — this demo uses the latter. At scale, pixi.js or a regl point sprite layer.

Loop discipline — applies to every rAF animation in the product. An unconditional requestAnimationFrame loop never stops, so it keeps a core busy and drains battery even while the animation is paused or scrolled out of sight. Any continuous loop MUST suspend when paused, when document.hidden is true, and when its view is not on screen — then resume on visibilitychange or an explicit call. Prefer that to a polling timer, which merely trades a 60 Hz wake-up for a slower one. This demo does exactly that; read the source for the pattern.

Spec language

"NOT RECOMMENDED. Do not implement for the operational dashboard."

Why not: continuous motion implies continuous throughput. Samples do not flow — they sit in a −80 °C freezer for weeks. Animating them as a stream would make a stalled pipeline look busy, which is the exact opposite of what UC2 needs the user to notice. If it is wanted for a lobby screen or a poster loop, scope it there explicitly and keep it out of the tool.

10.5 Micro-interaction & perceived performance

X9
Skeleton loading and value tweening
skeleton screen · shimmer · optimistic UI · number interpolation · perceived performance · layout stability (CLS)
What it is

A skeleton occupying the exact final geometry, then values counting up to their target. It makes a 1.5 s load feel shorter than a 0.8 s spinner, because the user's eye has somewhere to rest and the layout never jumps.

Reserving the final size is not cosmetic — it is what keeps cumulative layout shift at zero.

Implement with

CSS @keyframes gradient sweep for the shimmer; svelte/motion's tweened(0, {duration:800, easing: cubicOut}) for the counters.

Format numbers with Intl.NumberFormat, never string concatenation — Swedish locale users expect space as the thousands separator.

Spec language

"Any panel whose data is fetched MUST render a skeleton placeholder occupying the final layout geometry within 100 ms of navigation. Numeric KPIs MUST tween to their value over ≤800 ms. Cumulative layout shift on panel load MUST be 0. Counters MUST render the final value immediately under prefers-reduced-motion."

X10
Motion vocabulary — easing vs spring
easing curve · cubic-bezier · spring physics (stiffness, damping, mass) · overshoot · duration budget
What it is

Four ways to move the same distance. Linear reads as mechanical. Cubic-out is the safe default for UI. Cubic-in-out suits layout transitions where both ends matter. Spring overshoots and settles — it feels physical, but the overshoot is wrong for anything where the final position carries meaning, which in a graph is everything.

Implement with

svelte/motion: tweened(v,{easing: cubicOut}) and spring(v,{stiffness:0.15, damping:0.8}). d3-ease for the named curves. CSS cubic-bezier(0.33, 1, 0.68, 1) is cubic-out.

Note for the team: framer-motion and visx are React-only and do not apply to our SvelteKit stack. The Svelte-native equivalents above cover the same ground.

Spec language

"Default easing for all UI motion is cubic-out at 200–300 ms. Layout transitions use cubic-in-out at 500–800 ms. Spring physics MUST NOT be used where final position encodes data. No single transition may exceed 1000 ms except an explicitly user-triggered narrative animation (X2)."

10.8 Motion budget and accessibility — non-negotiables

  • prefers-reduced-motion: reduce MUST be honoured globally, not per component. One top-level store; every transition duration reads from it. This is a WCAG 2.1 requirement (2.3.3 Animation from Interactions), not a nicety — vestibular disorders are common and the audience is a hospital.
  • Nothing animates for longer than 1 s except a user-triggered narrative animation, which must be skippable.
  • Motion never carries information alone. If a node pulses because it is stalled, it must also be shaped, coloured and labelled as stalled. Animation is emphasis, never encoding.
  • Every canvas view has a table equivalent (report §6.2, mockup findings F13/F18) exposed to screen readers. For the SVG views, <title> and <desc> per node plus role="img" and an aria-label summary on the container.
  • Keyboard parity for the graph: roving tabindex across nodes, arrow keys to move between neighbours, Enter to expand, Esc to reset. Announce the selection through an aria-live="polite" region.
  • Hit targets ≥ 24×24 px (WCAG 2.2, 2.5.8). A 5 px node circle needs an invisible padded hit area.
  • Throttle pointer handlers to requestAnimationFrame, and batch graph mutations in cy.batch(). Most "the graph is laggy" bug reports are one of these two.

Cytoscape is 2D — the honest bit

You asked to include the 3D rotating graph "in Cytoscape." Cytoscape.js cannot do 3D — it is a strictly 2D library, with no depth, camera or rotation. Decision

WebGL + three.js is a heavy dependency (~1 MB). Justified for a lobby/poster attract loop; hard to justify inside the operational bundle. Code-split it behind a dynamic import and load it only when attract mode is entered — exactly as this tab loads it only when you open it.
